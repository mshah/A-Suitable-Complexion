THE MIRROR AND THE MARKET

Who is the fairest?

“Mirror, mirror on the wall, who's the fairest of them all?”

It is a question many of us encounter as children. In Snow White, the mirror is asked not simply to reflect a face, but to judge it. Beauty becomes a competition. Someone must be the fairest, and everyone else must fall somewhere below.

The fairy tale belongs to a different cultural history from India's matrimonial complexion classifications. Yet both reveal a familiar impulse: to turn beauty into a hierarchy and to present that hierarchy as though it were natural.

In matrimonial biodata, the mirror becomes a description: very fair, fair, wheatish, dark. In the beauty industry, it becomes a product recommendation. A person is shown an ideal and offered something to bring them closer to it.

Try the mirror: assign yourself a number, change it, and consider what has actually changed. More Flat and Gallery colour grading are selected, with optional switches for comparison.

RETURN TO THE FAMILY

What remains
after the ranking?

A Suitable Complexion begins with family photographs.

Weddings. Celebrations. People gathered together, dressed in the clothes they have chosen for an important occasion. The photographs record relationships, affection, cultural traditions, and moments worth remembering.

The paintings preserve much of this beauty. The intricate embroidery of a lehenga, the brilliant colours of a sari, the patterns woven into a sherwani, the jewellery and flowers. Each detail expresses something about the person wearing it: taste, culture, identity, individuality.

But the skin is treated differently.

Where the clothing celebrates difference, the complexion chart reduces it. Seven colours. Seven labels. A number assigned to a person.

And even those numbers cannot be trusted to remain consistent. The same individual appears as one complexion in one painting and another elsewhere. The chart remains unchanged; its judgments do not.

The history surrounding these paintings reveals how something as intimate as the desire to be loved, accepted, or considered beautiful can become a commercial opportunity. For generations, advertisements have offered lighter skin as a route to confidence, marriage, employment, and success.

The industry did not invent every prejudice it profits from. But it learned to sell products within those prejudices, sometimes reinforcing the very hierarchies that make its promises attractive.

This is the commercialization of inequality: transforming a social judgment into an individual problem, then offering a purchasable solution.

Yet the people in these paintings were never numbers to begin with.

They are daughters, sons, mothers, fathers, sisters, brothers, partners, and friends. Their relationships existed before the chart was imposed upon them. Their worth does not depend on where they fall within it.

THE QUESTION AT THE HEART OF THE WORK

Why should someone have to purchase what should never have been conditional—dignity, possibility, and belonging?

AFTER THE CHART

Perhaps the question was never who is the fairest of them all.

Perhaps the question is why we needed to choose at all.
