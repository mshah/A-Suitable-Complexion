# The Violence of a Neutral Chart

*A Suitable Complexion* begins with photographs that were not taken to make an argument. They were taken because someone had married, because relatives had gathered, because a holiday offered an evening of good light, or simply because there was a camera and there were people who wished to remember one another. Their historical value lies in their specificity. A person's smile, a relative's hand on someone else's shoulder, a dress kept for a special occasion: these are fragments of lives that exceed any system of classification.

Then a chart is introduced.

The chart has seven numbered colours. The names are not invented from nothing: they echo the categories documented on an Indian matrimonial website in 2017, including a series of increasingly modified versions of the word *wheatish*. The digital colours and the rule for assigning them are invented by the artist. A single point at the middle of a person's nose determines a flat fill for every part of their visible skin. Other figures are left unpainted, bearing only the number that would complete them. The same key appears beside every image. [S01] [S23]

At first, the intervention can seem like a game. Paint-by-numbers promises order: a complex scene can be made manageable if each shape is attached to the correct square in a legend. Here, the method becomes less innocent. It turns a family photograph into something that resembles a form to be completed. A person is fully there—face, clothing, posture, history—and still treated as if one descriptor could tell us what matters.

The chart's visual authority is counterfeit. A numbered legend borrows from the language of objectivity, but the categories reflect social habits, not scientific divisions among people. The colours are approximations selected for an artwork. Even the point of sampling is arbitrary. Its consistency is important because it reveals a troubling possibility: an arbitrary system can feel reasonable if it is applied with enough precision.

The series title invokes Vikram Seth's *A Suitable Boy*, a novel in which family expectation, marriage and choice are complicated by social context. The word *suitable* is almost exquisitely mild. It sounds sensible, considerate, practical. It can also conceal a judgment made before a person has been encountered on their own terms. In matrimonial contexts, complexion has been one of the criteria used to make such judgments, alongside considerations of class, caste, religion and family status. It would be a mistake to assume identical practices across every family or region; equally, it would be a mistake to pretend the language is harmless. Research on Indian matrimonial advertisements and preferences documents the persistence of fairness descriptors and the gendered pressures attached to them. [S11] [S12] [S24]

It is important that the photographs are not anonymous. These are relatives, not interchangeable models. The social story of colourism is often told through the spectacular advertising image—the dramatically transformed face, the smiling woman whose life improves, the product promising change. *A Suitable Complexion* starts instead with people whose value is already apparent in their relationships. The viewer can see affection before seeing classification. This order matters.

The paintings do not suggest that people in the photographs endorse the social categories imposed upon them. Nor do they identify any person's caste, ethnicity, worth or authentic biological colour. The work exposes the act of categorising. The blank figures are not missing family members, and they are not people without skin. Their incompletion is an interruption—a moment at which the viewer becomes conscious of being asked to finish a system.

Yet this is not only a family story. Skin colour has been marketed as a product problem, and there is a long history behind the sale. In late colonial India, international manufacturers tied skin creams to cleanliness, modernity, beauty and eventually the explicit language of whitening. Indian brands also localized these ideals; both imported prestige and national authenticity could be made to sell a pale ideal. The industry thus did not need to invent an insecurity entirely by itself. It could borrow from social arrangements already in place, intensify them and circulate them back as advertisements. [S02] [S03] [S04]

Later commercials connected complexion to marriage, ambition and work. One of the most revealing pieces of evidence is the Advertising Standards Council of India's 2014 guidance: it explicitly warns advertisers not to depict dark-skinned people as inferior or unsuccessful in matrimony, job placement, promotion or attraction. The guideline describes, in the negative, the visual story that the market had learned to tell. [S08]

Still, commercial storytelling is not proof that fairness causes these outcomes. A 2022 experimental hiring study in India found no statistically significant preference for lighter-skinned candidate photographs in its particular design. That finding does not erase colourism elsewhere, including matrimonial judgments, but it keeps the exhibit from mistaking an advertisement's claim for an established economic law. The extraordinary thing is that a promise can help sell a product even when the relationship between complexion and success is contested or context-dependent. [S10]

In the modern market, the vocabulary has changed. Unilever rebranded Fair & Lovely as Glow & Lovely in 2020 and publicly described a shift towards glow, even tone and radiance, while maintaining that its product is not a skin-bleaching cream. The transition deserves attention without flattening all pigmentation treatment into colourism. Treating a medically bothersome dark spot is not the same act as telling someone their inherited complexion reduces their worth. [S09]

The artwork and the research therefore work in opposite directions. The paintings compress intimate people into categories; the essays open those categories again, showing their historical construction, their commercial circulation and their contested meaning. No one in these photographs needs permission from a colour key to belong to the picture. That is what makes the key so disturbing.

**Pull quote:** The chart does not discover a hierarchy. It demonstrates how easily a hierarchy can be made to look like information.
