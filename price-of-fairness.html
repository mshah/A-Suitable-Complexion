Evidence and qualification

What the Label Cannot Tell You

The harms associated with skin-lightening are not all the same. There is the social harm of telling people that their natural complexion needs correction. There are also the medical harms of certain products, particularly illegal, mislabelled or adulterated preparations. Conflating these two claims would weaken the argument: not every cream marketed for glow contains hazardous substances, and dermatologists can legitimately treat disorders of pigmentation. The most serious physical risks described here concern particular ingredients, concentrations, misuse and regulatory failures. S15 S16 S17

Mercury: harm beyond the mirror

Some skin-lightening creams and soaps have contained mercury compounds that interfere with pigment production. Mercury exposure can damage the nervous system and kidneys. The WHO identifies mercury-containing skin-lightening cosmetics as a public-health problem and notes that banned or restricted products may continue to circulate through informal markets and the internet. In 2023, parties to the Minamata Convention agreed to remove the older 1 part-per-million threshold in the treaty's cosmetics phase-out framework, making explicit the goal that mercury should not be intentionally added to cosmetics. S15 S18

Hydroquinone: a medicine, not an unregulated cosmetic shortcut

Hydroquinone is a dermatological treatment for some hyperpigmentation conditions when prescribed and monitored appropriately, subject to national law. It is not permitted as an ordinary cosmetic ingredient in the UK, and the US FDA does not approve over-the-counter skin-lightening products containing it. Prolonged or inappropriate use is associated with irritation and in some cases exogenous ochronosis: persistent blue-black or grey-blue pigmentation—the opposite of the promised result. S16 S19

Potent corticosteroids: a medical drug with risks

Clobetasol is an extremely potent prescription corticosteroid used for inflammatory skin disease, not a routine cosmetic ingredient. Misuse can thin the skin and, with sufficient exposure, cause serious systemic adverse effects, including adrenal suppression. In December 2025, a UK border alert documented a 'toning' lotion containing both hydroquinone and clobetasol, and rejected the import. The alert is a specific tested product, not evidence that all imported creams are contaminated. S17 S22

The difficult market to regulate

In February 2026, the UK's Chartered Trading Standards Institute warned of illegal skin-lightening products sold online and in unexpected shops, including specialist food retailers. Product-safety alerts reveal why a label may be unreliable: testing can find prohibited ingredients regardless of the product's marketing description. This is a consumer-safety and supply-chain issue; it should not be used to stigmatise ethnic shops or the communities that shop there. S20 S22

What the exhibition does not claim

This website does not treat niacinamide, vitamin C, sunscreen or medically supervised treatments for hyperpigmentation as synonymous with toxic bleaching. It does not assume that every rebranded product has the same formula as before, or that every online listing is illicit. It does insist that people have the right to accurate claims, safe products and freedom from beauty advertising that makes social belonging appear contingent on skin shade.

Pull quote: The body can carry the cost of a promise that the advertisement never had to prove.

Health-information note: This is an explanation of evidence, not treatment advice. For suspected exposure to an illegal product or skin symptoms, consult a qualified clinician and the relevant local regulator.
